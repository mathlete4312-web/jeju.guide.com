01

History

Matthew
⚔

Jeju Island's history stretches back thousands of years to the ancient kingdom of Tamna, an independent island state that existed as early as 2000 BCE. Tamna maintained its own culture, language, and political identity, trading with mainland Korea, China, and Japan. The kingdom formally submitted to the Goryeo Dynasty in 938 CE but retained a degree of self-governance for centuries. During the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, Jeju served as a Mongol horse-breeding station, a legacy still visible in the island's horse-raising traditions (Kim, 2018).

One of the darkest chapters in Jeju's history is the April 3rd Massacre (Jeju 4.3 Incident) of 1948. Following the end of Japanese colonial rule in 1945, leftist uprisings were met with violent suppression by South Korean authorities and U.S.-backed forces. Between 1948 and 1954, an estimated 14,000 to 30,000 people — up to one-tenth of Jeju's population — were killed. Entire villages were burned to the ground. For decades the massacre was a forbidden topic; the South Korean government issued a formal apology only in 2003. Today, the Jeju 4.3 Peace Park memorializes the victims and stands as a symbol of the island's resilience (Jeju 4.3 Peace Foundation, 2021).

Jeju April 3rd Massacre — A pivotal anti-communist suppression campaign that killed tens of thousands of civilians. Recognition of the massacre was legally established under the Jeju April 3rd Incident Investigation and Victims Honoring Special Act (2000).

02

Geography

Gloria
🏔

Jeju Island is a volcanic island located in the Korea Strait, approximately 100 km south of the Korean Peninsula. Covering an area of 1,849 km², it is South Korea's largest island. The island was formed through a series of volcanic eruptions over millions of years, giving it a dramatic landscape of lava tubes, basalt cliffs, and over 368 parasitic volcanic cones known as oreum. At its center rises Hallasan (1,950 m), the highest peak in South Korea, which is classified as a shield volcano and is home to a stunning crater lake called Baengnokdam (UNESCO, 2007).

Jeju holds a rare UNESCO Triple Crown: it is simultaneously a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve (2002), a UNESCO World Natural Heritage Site (2007), and a UNESCO Global Geopark (2010). The island's natural wonders include the Geomunoreum lava tube system, Seongsan Ilchulbong (Sunrise Peak) — a tuff cone rising 182 m from the sea — and the old-growth forests of Hallasan National Park. Jeju's climate is subtropical, with mild winters and hot, rainy summers, supporting a rich biodiversity including species found nowhere else on Earth (Korea National Park Service, 2019).

Resource Example

Volcanic Island — Jeju's volcanic origin shapes everything from its soil (fertile for citrus farming) to its tourism industry (lava tube caves, Hallasan hiking). The island's geology is central to understanding its ecology and economy.

03

Economy

Seongyo
🍊

Jeju's economy has historically been rooted in agriculture and fishing. The island is famous for its hallabong (a premium mandarin orange hybrid), tangerines, and black pigs raised in traditional outdoor pens. Fishing — particularly abalone, sea cucumbers, and fish caught by the famous haenyeo divers — has sustained coastal communities for centuries. However, over the past three decades, tourism has become the dominant economic driver. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, Jeju welcomed approximately 15 million visitors per year, making tourism responsible for more than 20% of the island's GDP (Jeju Special Self-Governing Province, 2020).

In 2002, the South Korean government launched the Jeju Free International City initiative, aiming to transform Jeju into a globally competitive hub for business, education, and healthcare. As part of this plan, the Jeju Free Economic Zone offers tax incentives, deregulated land use, and streamlined investment procedures to attract foreign companies. The island has also invested heavily in renewable energy, targeting 100% clean energy by 2030 using wind and solar power, in line with Jeju's brand as a green, sustainable destination (Korea Energy Agency, 2021).

Brief Industry Overview — Jeju's economy spans primary sector (citrus, fishing), tertiary sector (tourism, hospitality), and emerging sectors (tech investment zones, renewable energy). Tourism revenue fluctuates significantly with geopolitical relations, particularly with China.

04

Government & Politics

Gov't Section
🏭

Jeju holds a unique political status within South Korea as the Jeju Special Self-Governing Province, established in 2006 under the Jeju Special Self-Governing Province Establishment and International Free City Development Special Act. Unlike South Korea's other provinces, Jeju has significantly greater autonomy: it abolished its county-level governments (replacing them with two administrative cities), gained authority over immigration and land-use policy, and established its own special economic regulations. The province is governed by an elected Governor and a unicameral provincial assembly (Ministry of the Interior and Safety, 2006).

A cornerstone of Jeju's political identity is the Jeju Special Economic Zone (SEZ), which grants the island the power to offer foreign investors preferential tax treatment, relaxed regulations on foreign schools and hospitals, and visa-free entry for many nationalities. Politically, the island has been a competitive battleground between South Korea's conservative and progressive parties. Environmental policy is increasingly central to local politics, as residents debate the costs of mass tourism, overdevelopment, and the influx of mainland Korean and Chinese real estate investors (Oh, 2019).

Resource Example

Special Economic Zone — Jeju's SEZ is designed to attract foreign direct investment through tax exemptions (up to 100% for qualifying businesses), English-medium education zones, and streamlined customs procedures — positioning Jeju as an "Asian Hub" similar to Singapore or Hong Kong.

05

Demographics

Eunseo
👥

As of the 2020 census, Jeju Island had a registered population of approximately 675,000 people, making it the least-populous of South Korea's nine provinces but one of the fastest-growing. The island's population grew by roughly 11% between 2015 and 2020, driven largely by an influx of migrants from Seoul and other major mainland cities seeking a quieter lifestyle. Jeju City, the provincial capital, accounts for about 490,000 residents, while Seogwipo, the southern administrative city, holds the remainder. The population density is approximately 365 people per km² (Statistics Korea, 2020).

Jeju's demographics reflect broader Korean trends: an aging population in rural areas contrasts with a younger urban core in Jeju City. The median age on the island is around 43 years. Jeju also has a growing multicultural population: foreign residents (mainly from China, Vietnam, and Southeast Asia) account for approximately 4% of the population as of 2020. Notably, Jeju saw a dramatic spike in housing costs during the 2010s as mainlanders and foreign investors purchased land, pricing out many native Jejuans and creating significant social tension (Statistics Korea, 2020; Yonhap News Agency, 2021).

06

Culture: Festivals, Food & Activities

Penelope
🏬

Jeju Island is celebrated for its rich cultural traditions, many of which are tied to its natural environment. The Jeju Fire Festival (Jeongwol Daeboreum), held on the first full moon of the lunar new year, is one of Korea's most spectacular events: residents burn the dry grass on the oreum slopes to ward off pests and welcome spring, creating a breathtaking display of flame across the hillsides. Thousands of visitors attend annually. Equally iconic is the tradition of the haenyeo — female free divers who harvest abalone, sea urchins, and other seafood by diving to depths of up to 20 meters without oxygen tanks. Recognized as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2016, the haenyeo culture represents female empowerment and environmental stewardship (UNESCO, 2016).

Jeju's culinary identity is inseparable from its landscape. Jeju black pork (heukdwaeji), raised outdoors on the island's volcanic pastures, is grilled tableside and considered a national delicacy. Hallabong, a sweet mandarin orange grown on Hallasan's slopes, is exported across Asia. Outdoor activities abound: the Jeju Olle Trail, a network of 437 km of coastal and inland walking paths, draws hikers year-round. Jeju's push for sustainable tourism — including plastic-free beach campaigns, eco-certified guesthouses, and visitor caps at sensitive natural sites — reflects a growing awareness that the island's beauty must be protected for future generations (Jeju Tourism Organization, 2022).

Resource Example

Sustainable Tourism — Jeju's tourism board launched the "Jeju Green" initiative in 2019, incentivizing eco-friendly accommodations and reducing single-use plastics across 450 participating businesses. Visitor footprint data is tracked at Hallasan National Park to prevent ecological damage.
